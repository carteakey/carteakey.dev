For the last few days I've watched from the sidelines while everyone and their grandma "hacked" and dumbed down their TVs. That mostly meant Android-based OSes: swap the launcher for a custom one, run a few ADB commands to trim the bloat. {% annotate "said the man with the un-hackable TV", "right" %}Rookie stuff{% endannotate %}, I told myself, with the sour-grapes energy of someone stuck owning a Hisense TV running VIDAA OS. I hated it so much I'd bought a Fire Stick to replace it (and then did the same ADB debloating on the stick).

VIDAA is Hisense's proprietary, locked-down Linux. There's no ADB and no developer mode. And it had two gripes that bothered me to no end:

- **Relentless telemetry** - I'd disabled every tracking toggle in the menus and it still phoned home.
- **Refusing to default to the last input** - no matter what setting I flipped, turning the TV on landed on the ad-infested VIDAA home screen instead of going straight to my Fire TV or PC.

Seeing all the RE writeups, decomp drama, and clones this week made me wonder: given enough free time, tokens (thanks, Global build of Z Code), and a willing[^1] chinese[^2] model like GLM 5.3 Flash, could I get root shell access on this thing? That'd give me a higher sense (Hisense) of the TV, let me finally say *al(vidaa)* (goodbye in Urdu - hence the repo name) to the stock bloat, and open up two real tracks beyond run-of-the-mill ADB:

- a custom home launcher that the TV installs itself
- actual root access for firewalling telemetry and modifying the firmware and boot UI

And that I did. ~300M tokens and a couple hours of running recons on the bridge later, I finally got the message from our friendly chinese model. Everything lives in [carteakey/alvidaa](https://github.com/carteakey/alvidaa), and here's how the whole chain worked.

## The wall that stops everyone

My spec for the launcher came first, and I never changed it: **a clock, my HDMI inputs, and my real apps - nothing else.** I wanted something functional, not fancy. A launcher can't be enshittified if it doesn't have a feed.

The control mechanism already existed before I started. VIDAA TVs expose a **RemoteNow** channel (MQTT over TLS on port 36669), which is what the official Hisense phone remote app uses. If you publish an action there, the TV obeys: launch an app, switch inputs, or inject remote keys. I'd planned to build a small web page plus a lightweight Python server on my Raspberry Pi that speaks RemoteNow. Many of the guides I found used static credentials from 2018.

On my modern firmware, the TV answered those credentials with a popup on screen:

> *"Your TV mode is no longer compatible with the version of the app. Please try with the latest version."*

The broker returned an authorization error, and every existing guide was dead in the water. I found the first clue buried in an issue thread on an old repo: newer firmware checks the **client certificate's identity at the MQTT layer** and only accepts the certificate bundled inside the current official VIDAA Smart TV Android app (`CN=VidaaAppAndroidV01`). Since APK mirrors block automated downloads, I pulled the package on my phone, AirDropped it to my Mac, dug the keystore out of the app resources, and extracted the certificate with OpenSSL's legacy PKCS12 mode. For the first time, the TV accepted my TLS handshake.

The certificate alone still wasn't enough. Modern firmware also demands dynamic per-connection credentials: an MD5 digest of the TV's MAC address, a username XORed against a hardcoded constant, and a password salted by the digit sum of the current Unix timestamp. Someone at Hisense clearly had fun designing that handshake.

Then came the pairing ritual. The TV pops up a four-digit PIN on screen for you to send back over MQTT. My initial keepalive loop - meant to hold the dialog open while I typed - re-triggered the pairing request every few seconds, and **every re-trigger rotates the PIN**. Five codes died on that hill before I realized what was going on: you've got to trigger once, wait, and then submit. I also hit a second trap where I could publish fine and didn't hear a word back, because this firmware silently rejects wildcard MQTT subscriptions. Once I switched to subscribing to exact topic strings, the TV started talking back.

With pairing finished, I'd captured the long-lived tokens (a two-day access token and a thirty-day refresh token, so you only pair once) and the bridge was mine: app lists, input switching, key injection, and power state.

## A TV that installs its own launcher

I built the launcher itself as a single HTML page drawn on one flat canvas. Drawing everything onto a single canvas keeps rendering fast and predictable on the TV's browser without fighting CSS compositing bugs. In the initial setup, the Raspberry Pi pushed configuration and live HDMI signal state over a WebSocket. If the Pi restarted, the clock kept ticking on screen and reconnected on its own.

{% image_cc "./src/static/img/alvidaa/first-light-tv.jpg", "Photo of the TV showing the custom alvidaa launcher at 15:51 with a clock, HDMI inputs, and app tiles", "w-3/4", "First light at 15:51 - clock, inputs, and apps. Also visible: the SETUP tile rendered white-on-white and the remote's on-screen pointer. Two bugs I didn't know I was photographing." %}

The best part was how it installs. The classic hidden installer URL from older posts (`hisense://debug`) doesn't work on this firmware. While reading through a newer VIDAA web-app repo, I found something much better: **the TV browser exposes its internal bridge functions to any web page it opens.**

When my launcher page loads, it detects those globals (`HiUtils_createRequest`) and renders an install tile on screen. Clicking it makes the TV read its own app registry JSON (`websdk/Appinfo.json`), append the launcher to the list, and write the file back. After one restart, "Home" shows up as a native TV app. The TV had installed its own replacement launcher from a plain HTTP visit.

> "woah. i installed and it shows up and works. how can we make this the default app and there is so much to do now… lets branch this whole thing out as a separate repo… and document findings so far like the cert thing and what not. so so much work hahaa lets"

That's when I spun it out into **alvidaa** and started keeping a lab notebook in [docs/findings/](https://github.com/carteakey/alvidaa/tree/main/docs/findings) where every discovery, trap, and error code got written down the moment it happened. That notebook's the only reason the second half of the weekend worked.

To finish the first version of the launcher, I added a watcher on the Pi that relaunches my Home app whenever the stock UI appears (leaving about a second of stock screen flash, which is the ceiling when you don't have root), a config allowlist that hides preinstalled junk apps, an icon proxy that caches app logos from the TV's CDN, and a systemd service on the Pi. It was already a solid appliance - and then I got curious.

## The same door, walked further

Later that evening, I sent the prompt that turned a custom launcher project into a root project:

> "look online if firmware hacks exist for vidaa os and see if you can come up with a backdoor to gain root access to my tv"

I couldn't find any published root method for modern VIDAA firmware. [Bananamafia's hisensehax](https://bananamafia.dev/post/hisensehax/) was a great earlier reference for how the browser APIs work, though it stopped short of root. (Everything below was tested on my specific set, a Hisense 50A51HUV running a MediaTek MTK9602 SoC; other models and firmware builds will need their own testing.)

We weren't starting from scratch, though. That same browser bridge that'd let my launcher edit the app registry is a general file read/write API exposed to JavaScript. Think of it like a hotel room phone wired to the front desk: it's only supposed to reach one small folder, not the rest of the building.

To see what else it could reach, we built a self-reporting recon page that runs a batch of checks in the TV browser and posts the JSON results back to the Pi. I even installed Recon as a native TV app so it sat right in the stock home screen's Recent row, next to *The Mummy* on Tubi:

{% image_cc "./src/static/img/alvidaa/recon-app-tv-home.jpg", "Stock VIDAA home screen showing the Recon app tile in the Recent row", "w-3/4", "The recon probe installed as a first-class TV app. Recent: Recon, sitting right above the billboard it was built to replace." %}

We walked the bridge in four steps, and at every step the TV politely told us where to go next:

1. **Who are you?** The TV runs on a MediaTek armv7 SoC, and its app browser ("odin") is actually Chromium 111 rather than the ancient Chrome build that older guides assumed.
2. **Can we leave the sandbox?** When you ask a program for a file it can't open, its error message often includes the full path it tried. We asked the file reader for `/etc/passwd`, and the error came back: `open("/APPS//etc/passwd") -> ENOENT`. That single error gave away the entire sandbox: the bridge simply glues `/APPS/` onto the front of every path. So we asked for `../etc/passwd` ("go up one folder"), and it walked straight out to the real root filesystem. Even better, SELinux is in permissive mode because the boot script explicitly disables enforcement.

{% image_cc "./src/static/img/alvidaa/traversal-write-works.jpg", "TV screen showing recon v2 output: TRAVERSAL WRITE WORKS - root lane open", "w-3/4", "recon v2 live on the TV screen: TRAVERSAL WRITE WORKS - root lane open, in yellow. The sandbox confessed its base path, and ../ walked right out." %}

3. **Who runs the bridge?** The JavaScript functions in the browser are thin wrappers that post JSON to a local helper on `localhost:9009`. That helper is **phoenix**, a Node.js server running as an unprivileged service user whose own source code sits on the writable `/APPS` partition. While the browser wrappers stamp every request with a rotating secret token (like a wax seal that changes each time), we replayed a raw request without the stamp and the helper answered anyway - with wide-open CORS. The bouncer checked wax seals at the front door; the back door had no lock and a welcome mat. Read-only system partitions are protected by dm-verity, so we leave those alone, while `/APPS`, `/data`, `/var/local`, and the OTA staging partition are all read-write.
4. **Is there a maintenance door?** Since the bridge user isn't root, we used our new read access to inspect the TV's boot scripts. Inside `rc.local` - which runs as **root** on every boot - we found this block sitting in shipping firmware:

```sh
if [ -e /var/local/otaTest.sh ]; then
    chmod +x /var/local/otaTest.sh
    /var/local/otaTest.sh &
fi
```

{% image_cc "./src/static/img/alvidaa/root-achieved-rclocal.png", "Screenshot of rc.local showing the /var/local/otaTest.sh execution block", "w-3/4", "The moment it landed: an OTA auto-test script on a partition the browser bridge can write. Root = write one file and reboot." %}

Because `/var/local` is world-writable at boot, the browser bridge can drop files directly into it. **Writing `/var/local/otaTest.sh` gives arbitrary code execution as root on the next boot.** To make iteration effortless from the couch, I added a one-line endpoint on the Pi that tells the TV to load any URL over our existing MQTT session. New recon and hook versions could install themselves remotely without touching the physical remote.

Then came the comedy. We wrote the first root hook, rebooted the TV with the remote, and waited. Nothing happened. When the recon report came back, `/proc/uptime` showed the TV had been running for over a full day. Pressing power on the remote only puts the TV into standby, and standby doesn't re-run `rc.local`.

{% annotate "some lessons still require physically unplugging the television", "left" %}Pull the wall plug for 30 seconds.{% endannotate %}

{% image_cc "./src/static/img/alvidaa/the-plug.jpg", "Hand holding the unplugged TV power cord", "w-1/2", "The highest-leverage thirty seconds of the project." %}

Half a minute of darkness later, the TV booted fresh and handed us a smoking gun: `/var/local/otaTest.log` existed, and our unprivileged bridge got `Permission denied` trying to read it. Our hook had made its output dumps world-readable and forgotten to change permissions on its own log file. Because the log file was owned by root, that permission error proved the script had run as root.

**Root was confirmed.** The world-readable dumps came back through the bridge with the full process map, listening ports, `/etc/shadow`, and the root-only serial console gate scripts.

## Getting an interactive shell (the five-lock gauntlet)

Running a blind script at boot is great, and what I really wanted was an interactive SSH shell from my laptop. The [root guide](https://github.com/carteakey/alvidaa/blob/main/docs/ROOT.md) and [findings modules](https://github.com/carteakey/alvidaa/tree/main/docs/findings) document every command in detail; here's how we got from boot-time script execution to an interactive root login.

First, we checked the toolbox on disk. The firmware doesn't ship an SSH server, and its built-in BusyBox was compiled without `nc`, `telnetd`, `wget`, or `httpd`. Thankfully, `/bin/sh` is actually full GNU bash, and bash has `/dev/tcp/<host>/<port>` built right in. That gave us a zero-binary TCP client to pull tools off the Raspberry Pi at boot.

{% image_cc "./src/static/img/alvidaa/busybox-devtcp.png", "Terminal output showing missing busybox network applets alongside working bash /dev/tcp", "w-3/4", "The toolbox inventory and the way out: no nc, telnetd, or wget in BusyBox, so bash /dev/tcp carried our static binaries over the LAN." %}

Because the read-only rootfs can't take new dynamic libraries, we cross-compiled a static **Dropbear** SSH server (`arm-linux-musleabihf`) using `zig cc` on my Mac and bundled it with a static BusyBox. Our next hook fetched that archive over `/dev/tcp` at boot (with a retry loop, since the boot script starts a few seconds before Wi-Fi connects) and launched Dropbear.

The boot log reported that Dropbear was listening with host keys generated - and yet SSH from my laptop timed out. A timeout can have several causes, including dropped packets. Our boot dump of the firewall rules explained what was happening here: **Hisense ships an anti-tamper firewall (`FIREWALL_IN_EULAON`) on the TV.**

The firewall allows incoming traffic on the RemoteNow MQTT port, ICMP, and loopback, drops everything else, and explicitly blacklists debug ports including SSH, Telnet, and Chromium DevTools. It defended the wrong direction: the firewall watches the perimeter, and our attack never knocked from the outside. It walked in through the web browser, got root, and then - as the firewall's own administrator - read the blacklist with the firewall's own `iptables` and added our ports to the allow list. The building's security guard caught us, and then it turned out we'd hired him. After that, we cleared four more locks in a row:

| Lock | What happened | How we cleared it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Firewall (`FIREWALL_IN_EULAON`)** | Drops all inbound LAN traffic except MQTT/ICMP and blacklists ports 22, 23, 4444, 9222, 12321 | **Hook v5:** Insert `iptables -I INPUT 1` accept rules, re-applied in a 15s loop for the first 3 minutes of boot so EULA state scripts can't flush them |
| **2. Dropbear `-D` key path** | Dropbear now answered with `Permission denied (publickey)` because `-D <dir>` *replaces* `.ssh` instead of joining it | Placed `authorized_keys` directly at `/var/local/roothome/authorized_keys` |
| **3. Missing `/etc/shells`** | Dropbear's `checkusername()` checks the user's shell against `/etc/shells` before reading keys; `/etc/shells` doesn't exist and root's shell is `/usr/local/scripts/rootlogin.sh` | **Hook v6/v7:** Patched the shell-validity check out of our static Dropbear build |
| **4. World-writable `/var/local`** | `start-hisense.sh` runs `chmod 777 /var/local` at every boot, causing Dropbear's `checkpubkeyperms()` parent-directory walk to reject any key stored on `/var/local` | **Hook v8:** Patched `checkpubkeyperms()` out of our static Dropbear build |
| **5. USB gate wearing root's shell** | SSH finally connected, printed `1`, and exited! Root's login shell (`rootlogin.sh`) *is* the physical USB-key gate script (`key_check.sh`), which ran on login, failed its USB check with code `1`, and quit | **Hook v10:** Patched `get_user_shell()` in Dropbear to exec `/bin/sh` directly instead of `pw_shell` (after v9's `command="/bin/sh"` got mangled by shell escaping) |

{% image_cc "./src/static/img/alvidaa/ssh-gate-costume.png", "Terminal screenshot showing SSH connecting and immediately printing 1 before closing", "w-3/4", "Lock 5 in action: SSH connected, printed 1, and closed, because root's login shell in /etc/passwd was the factory USB-key check script." %}

SSH was fixed in hook v10. Running `ssh -p 2222 -i server/keys/tv_root_ed25519 root@<tv-ip>` lands at a root shell from my laptop on every boot. Hook v10 refreshes its tools from the Pi at startup, and a ten-line wrapper (`server/tv`) makes interactive shells and one-shot commands effortless from the couch. The wall plug hasn't been pulled since.

## What owning looks like

The honest map, because hype is cheap:

- **What we hold:** persistent boot-time root execution (remotely updatable over MQTT, with a rolling backup hook on the TV), an interactive key-only SSH shell, the file bridge as a local API, remote page loading, and remote reboots.
- **The wall that stands:** verified boot, locked and green. The read-only system partitions can't be modified - and don't need to be. The UI, the ads, and the apps all live on the writable partitions. The cage is real; everything valuable was stored outside it.
- **The road ahead:** inspect the firmware dumps, map the network traffic, and experiment with blocking ads using the TV's `iptables`. Then the real prize: move the launcher server *onto the TV* (there's a Node runtime sitting right there), retire the Pi, and remove the last ~1 second of stock UI.

Next, I proved out the firmware dump path by streaming the verity-protected rootfs straight to the Pi over `/dev/tcp` with `dd` while the TV was running normally:

```
hsqs  ->  SquashFS 4.0, LZO-compressed, 30.5 MB, built 2025-08-13
```

Unpacking that image on the Pi paid off right away. Sitting at `/etc/0001-cli-login-aes.pem` (world-readable inside the image) was the AES decryption key for Hisense's serial-debug CLI. It wasn't encrypted; it was just unreachable until we had root. And we didn't have to modify a single byte of the verified rootfs to get it.

Walking the rest of the extracted images turned up three great discoveries:

- **Loewe and CVTE bones under the hood:** Digging through `/usr/local/` revealed configuration files in **Loewe's** DCL format (referencing Loewe's system config paths and clone files), alongside service lists for **CVTE** and **Cultraview** and a database named after Hisense's Qingdao campus (`huangdao.db`). One multi-brand ODM platform powers all of them. CVTE even left factory logger scripts on disk showing how factory USB sticks remount the root and `/usr/local` partitions read-write.
- **The stock home UI is an editable webpack SPA:** On `/APPS/channelA/hisenseUI/`, the stock home screen is just a six-line HTML loader pulling JavaScript bundles into an embedded Cobalt web engine, launched at boot by `hisense_stage0.sh`. Normal boots don't overwrite the UI folder (the deploy script only re-extracts when an OTA upgrade flag file is present), which means we can shim the loader directly to boot straight into **alvidaa** with zero stock-UI flash.
- **The OTA switch file:** The upgrade config in `/OAD/` currently shows both upgrade and reset flags set to zero. Since that JSON file is integrity-checked and can be rewritten by the cloud service, blocking OTA endpoints via the firewall in our boot hook remains the real guardrail before we edit any UI files in place.

## Of course we ran Doom

Every rooted machine since 1993 owes the same tax. Reading `/etc/shadow` proves you can read; running Doom on the living room screen proves you own the box.

Our recipe (documented end to end in [docs/DOOM.md](https://github.com/carteakey/alvidaa/blob/main/docs/DOOM.md)):

- **[doomgeneric](https://github.com/ozkl/doomgeneric)**, which only asks you to implement five functions (init, draw frame, sleep, ticks, and key input).
- A hundred-line Linux framebuffer backend in C that opens `/dev/fb0`, memory-maps the buffer, and nearest-neighbor scales each frame to 1080p.
- Cross-compiled as a static ARM binary using the same Zig toolchain we'd used for Dropbear. The entire game binary is **365 KB** - smaller than a single wallpaper.
- Debian's freely redistributable shareware WAD.

Deploying it onto a stripped-down embedded Linux had its own charm: there's no `scp` or SFTP server (so we piped the binary and WAD over standard input through SSH), no `nohup` (our static BusyBox's `setsid` covered it), and the framebuffer device refuses standard `read()` calls, so we wrote a tiny `fbcat` helper using `mmap` to grab raw screenshots alongside phone photos:

Right now it runs the shareware attract demo on E1M1 while we wire the remote's input events into the key handler. After that, we can register a wrapper script in the app registry so Doom sits in the native app row right between YouTube and Spotify.

## The telemetry shelf

Early on I'd asked the agent to check what sort of telemetry games the TV plays when tracking is turned off. Between `netstat`, the process table, and a strings sweep of the main TV binary and UI bundle, we now have a concrete map:

| Layer | What we found on the TV |
| --- | --- |
| **Local reporting hub** | Almost every daemon (`tvmain`, `systemserver`, `hisenseUI`, `phoenix`, YouTube, `masman`, AirPlay, DLNA) holds an open socket to `hi_logreport_service` on `127.0.0.1:1969`, alongside `mdharvester`, `write_stat`, and `bizm`. |
| **Cloud endpoints** | ~30 `*.vidaahub.com` subdomains in `sdk.js` and `tvmain` (`layout-ui-eu`, `recommend-ui`, `file-dl-test.../promovod/`, dynamic log-upload hosts), plus `phoenix` holding a live TLS connection to Tencent Cloud (`43.157.127.133:10443`) and an unauthenticated Mosquitto broker on `0.0.0.0:1883`. |
| **DNS fallbacks** | `/etc/resolv.conf` lists my Pi (AdGuard Home), `1.1.1.1`, and three IPv6 resolvers. Whether a blocked lookup reaches another resolver depends on the response and client behavior. |
| **Localhost bridge risk** | `localhost:9009/service/hiutils` requires no auth and sends `Access-Control-Allow-Origin: *`, while the browser exposes silent app installation to any page. Don't browse the open web on the TV. |

To measure what actually leaves the TV before we start swing-blocking domains, we added persistent `iptables` and `ip6tables` logging rules in `/var/local/tvrc`, sourced by the boot hook. Once a few days of baseline connection and DNS logs accumulate with all privacy toggles "off", we'll lock in the kernel blocklist right inside the boot hook.

## What this opens up

Having a root shell means I can run my own programs on the TV, as long as they're built for its ARM hardware and can work with the libraries and devices available there. Doom is one example; it doesn't mean every Linux executable will run unchanged.

I can also investigate and modify the launcher and the stock home UI. The custom launcher already works; replacing the stock home at boot and changing its components are the next experiments, with backups in place first.

And then there's the telemetry. The logs can show where the TV connects and when, but they don't reveal the contents of encrypted traffic. I want to watch the baseline for a few days, work out which services are responsible, and share what I find in [docs/findings/](https://github.com/carteakey/alvidaa/tree/main/docs/findings). More on that in the next few days.

For now, it just said *alvida* to its ads.
